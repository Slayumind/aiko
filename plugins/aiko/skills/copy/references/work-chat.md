# Messages to colleagues in a work chat

A message in Slack or a group chat, written for the user and sent in their name. The reader is a
colleague in the middle of a discussion, often busy, sometimes annoyed. They judge the message by
one thing: did it answer what they asked. Style comes second.

It is fine that an agent helped to write the message, and the user may say so openly. Do not hide
it and do not suggest hiding it. A reader is put off by a text nobody read with human eyes, not by
the tool.

## Before you write

1. **Find the question.** Quote it to yourself in the colleague's own words. The first sentence of
   the message answers exactly that question.
2. **Search the thread first.** If the answer to your own question is already in the thread, do not
   ask it. A question the thread already answers reads as "they did not read us".
3. **Check what each number proves.** A number goes into the message only if you checked that it
   supports the claim next to it. A small or biased sample is not evidence; say what it cannot show,
   or leave it out. A claim you have to take back tomorrow costs more trust than a missing number.
4. **Check facts that others will check.** How a program works, who said what, what a document
   says. If you are not sure, ask the person who knows before you write it as a fact.

## The request and the answer

- **They asked for X, and you think X will not help.** Do not silently give Y instead. Name X first,
  say in one sentence why X will not help, then give Y and say how Y answers their question.
  Silent substitution reads as ignoring the request, and the person asks again, louder.

  Bad: the colleague asks for the unit economics of small groups; the reply is a table of group
  sizes and renewal rates, with no word about unit economics.

  Good: «Юнит-экономику отдельно не считаю: косты и ставки у этих групп такие же, как у других
  курсов, меняется только число учеников. Поэтому вот распределение групп по размеру — от него и
  зависит, в плюсе ли группа».

- **Someone else's position: quote it.** Do not retell a colleague's words in your own. If you
  cannot quote, ask. A wrong retelling ("as you wrote, they sold the wrong course") hurts more than
  any style problem.
- **A link comes after the answer, never instead of it.** "The steps are in section 6" is not an
  answer. Give the answer in one or two sentences, then the link for details.
- **One message, one point.** Do not close five open questions in one message. The main one gets
  lost, and every extra point is one more thing to argue with.

## Form

- **A conversation is not a report.** In a live discussion: no headings, no numbered sections, no
  bold labels, no "What we need from you" block. Write the way you would say it at the next desk.
- **Length.** In a dispute or a heated thread, up to about 600 characters. Something longer goes
  into a document; the chat gets two lines and a link.
- **Report form is fine for news nobody argues with**: a changelog, an updated list, a status post.
- **Hot thread, first move.** Acknowledge the point, propose a call or one next step. Do not answer
  irritation with a new portion of data.

## Mistakes

- Own a mistake once, in one sentence, and say what changes. No excuses ("we sent everything, it
  may have got lost"), no defensive framing ("so that there is no illusion that...").
- If a fact changed since your last message, say it directly: "Earlier I wrote X. The program
  passport says Y, so Z." Do not let the reader find the reversal on their own.

## Texts prepared for the user, not for the chat

When you prepare something for the user to read (a timeline, an analysis, a set of options), mark
it as such. A long working text pasted into a chat as a post reads as a wall. If the user wants to
post it, offer a short version for the chat and keep the long one as a document.

## Checklist before the user sends

- The first sentence answers the colleague's actual question.
- If the answer is not what was asked, the message says why.
- Every number was checked for what it proves.
- No retold positions, only quotes.
- No link used as the whole answer.
- In a dispute: under about 600 characters, no headings or numbered blocks.
- The text survived the paste: no cut words, no glued links.
